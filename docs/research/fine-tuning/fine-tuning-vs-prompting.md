# Agents should prompt, retrieve and measure first, and tune a model only for a measured gap that outlives its base model

Chapter T11 of the book needs a section on fine-tuning versus prompting (todo [T39](../../todos/done/T39-section-fine-tuning-vs-prompting.md)), and our repository says almost nothing about it ([repo notes](notes/our-repo.md)). The evidence gathered on 19 November 2026 holds a paradox. In the papers, tuning wins often: 310 models tuned with LoRA beat GPT-4 by 10 points on average across 31 tasks, in a report from Predibase, which sells tuning ([LoRA Land, arXiv 2405.00732v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00732)). In production tuning is rare. Fourteen of 20 teams interviewed for the MAP study used no fine-tuning or reinforcement learning at all, and the interviewed teams called both methods "brittle to model upgrades, requiring costly retraining when versions change" ([MAP, arXiv 2512.04123v4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04123), §5.2). The providers have pulled back as well: OpenAI closed its tuning service to new organisations in May 2026 and stops all new training jobs on 6 January 2027 ([OpenAI deprecations](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations)). In our reading, the papers measure one model on one task, while a deployed agent must keep working as its model is replaced, and that difference resolves the paradox.

Five findings answer the research question, with every version, price and status as of November 2026. First, tuning teaches a model behaviour (a format, a tone, a narrow skill), and in controlled studies it adds facts less well than retrieval does. Second, prompting reaches further than its reputation: careful prompts beat tuned specialist models in medicine, and a prompt optimiser beat reinforcement learning with up to 35 times fewer rollouts. Third, training itself is cheap. The cost lies in the higher price per token, in the data and evals, and above all in re-tuning when the base model retires. Tunable base models retired in 2026, or due to retire by January 2027, lasted 16 to 30 months. Fourth, OpenAI and Anthropic have all but left self-serve tuning, so in November 2026 Google is the only one of the three that sells tuning of its current models. Fifth, deployed agents mostly prompt and tune where latency, volume, domain knowledge or cost justifies it, and the vendors who train agent models with reinforcement learning do so inside their own harness, at a scale few teams can match. This report traces each finding to its sources, turns them into a decision rule, and ends with what Chapter T11's section should teach, the example it needs and proposed plan changes. Its thesis is that an agent builder should default to prompting, tools and evals, and tune only for a gap an eval proves, at a volume or latency that repays the training, on a base model that lives long enough to repay it.

## Eight terms are used one way throughout

**Prompting** means steering a model through what one request contains: instructions, examples, retrieved text and tool definitions. **Fine-tuning**, shortened to **tuning**, is any training that changes a model's weights after its provider released it; the report uses it as the umbrella term. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)** trains on example inputs paired with the outputs wanted, and **preference tuning** trains on pairs of a better and a worse output, as OpenAI's direct preference optimization (DPO) does. **Reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT)** trains on the scores a grader gives the model's own attempts; Google calls its version reinforcement learning fine-tuning (RLFT), and reinforcement learning (RL) names the general method when a vendor trains its own model. **Distillation** is SFT on a larger model's outputs, so that a smaller model learns one task from it. The **base model** is the model a tuning job starts from, and the **tuned model** is what the job produces.

## Tuning teaches a model behaviour more than facts, so in controlled studies knowledge belongs in retrieval

**Controlled studies find that tuning adds knowledge poorly, and that retrieval adds it better.** Ovadia et al. compared unsupervised fine-tuning with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) on knowledge-heavy tasks and found that "RAG consistently outperforms it, both for existing knowledge encountered during training and entirely new knowledge" ([arXiv 2312.05934v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.05934)). Gekhman et al. then showed a cost beyond weak recall. Training examples that carry new facts are "learned significantly slower", and once learned "they linearly increase the model's tendency to hallucinate". Their conclusion is that models "mostly acquire factual knowledge through pre-training, whereas fine-tuning teaches them to use it more efficiently" ([arXiv 2405.05904v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05904)). The two methods can also stack. In a Microsoft case study on agriculture, tuning raised accuracy by over 6 percentage points and RAG added 5 more ([arXiv 2401.08406v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08406)). OpenAI's own guide draws the same line as a matrix. "Context optimization" serves a model that "lacks contextual knowledge" or holds knowledge that "is out of date", while "LLM optimization" serves "inconsistent results with incorrect formatting", the wrong "tone or style", or reasoning "not being followed consistently" ([OpenAI, optimizing LLM accuracy](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/optimizing-llm-accuracy)).

**The providers agree on what tuning is for: formats, labels, style and a cheaper model for one task.** OpenAI lists SFT for "Classification", "Nuanced translation", "Generating content in a specific format" and "Correcting instruction-following failures" ([OpenAI, model optimization](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/model-optimization)). Google lists "Strict output formatting, classification, sentiment analysis, entity extraction, and domain-specific style or tone" ([Google, introduction to tuning](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/tuning)). OpenAI names four gains over prompting alone. A tuned model can learn from more examples than fit in one request, and it can run on "shorter prompts with fewer examples and context data". It can learn from proprietary data "without having to include it via examples in every request", and a "smaller, cheaper, faster model" can learn a task that a larger model handles at too high a cost ([OpenAI, model optimization](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/model-optimization)). Anthropic's published Claude 3 Haiku result fits the list: tuning the model to moderate forum comments raised classification accuracy from 81.5% to 99.6% and cut tokens per query by 85%, a vendor result on its own task ([Anthropic, 2024](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fine-tune-claude-3-haiku)).

**A small tuned model can match a large prompted one on the task it was trained for.** Hsieh et al. distilled the reasoning of a large model into a 770-million-parameter T5, which then beat the few-shot prompted 540-billion-parameter PaLM on one benchmark using 80% of the training data ([Distilling Step-by-Step, arXiv 2305.02301v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02301)). The LoRA Land models gained 34 points over their base models on average ([arXiv 2405.00732v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00732)). The cheaper tuning method learns less and forgets less. LoRA, which trains only low-rank changes to selected weight matrices, "substantially underperforms full finetuning" on code and maths, yet "better maintains the base model's performance on tasks outside the target domain" ([arXiv 2405.09673v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09673)).

**Tuning can also undo what the provider trained in, which prompting cannot.** Qi et al. broke GPT-3.5 Turbo's safety guardrails by tuning it on 10 adversarial examples costing less than $0.20 through OpenAI's API, and found that tuning on benign data "can also inadvertently degrade the safety alignment", though less ([arXiv 2310.03693v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03693)). Narrow data can change behaviour far from its topic. A model "finetuned to output insecure code without disclosing this to the user" then "acts misaligned on a broad range of prompts that are unrelated to coding" ([Emergent Misalignment, arXiv 2502.17424v7](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17424)). We conclude that a tuned model is a new model, which needs the same safety and regression evals as one, while a changed prompt leaves the provider's training in place.

## Prompting closes much of the gap, and agent tuning pays mostly where a small model must do a large model's job

**Careful prompting has matched or beaten tuning in controlled comparisons, including on agent trajectories.** Microsoft's Medprompt combined general prompting methods and made GPT-4 beat the tuned specialist Med-PaLM 2 "by a significant margin with an order of magnitude fewer calls", with a 27% lower error rate on MedQA than the best specialist result ([arXiv 2311.16452v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.16452)). Prompts with hundreds or thousands of examples perform "comparably to fine-tuning" on some tasks, though their "inference cost increases linearly" with the examples ([Many-Shot In-Context Learning, arXiv 2404.11018v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.11018)). When Mosbach et al. held the model and the examples fixed, few-shot tuning and in-context learning generalised "similarly", and both varied widely ([arXiv 2305.16938v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16938)). GEPA brings the comparison to agents. It samples a system's trajectories, "reasoning, tool calls, and tool outputs", reflects on them in natural language and rewrites the prompts. Across six tasks it beat the reinforcement-learning method GRPO "by 6% on average and by up to 20%, while using up to 35x fewer rollouts" ([arXiv 2507.19457v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457)).

**The agent-tuning papers show the reverse case: a small open model trained on a large model's trajectories.** FireAct tuned Llama2-7B on 500 agent trajectories generated by GPT-4 and raised its HotpotQA score by 77%, against agents that "rely on few-shot prompting techniques with off-the-shelf LMs" ([arXiv 2310.05915v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05915)). AgentTuning's 70B model became "comparable to GPT-3.5-turbo on unseen agent tasks" ([arXiv 2310.12823v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12823)). Agent distillation, which trains a small model on a teacher agent's full tool-using trajectories, let models of 0.5B to 3B parameters compete with the next size up trained on reasoning traces alone ([arXiv 2505.17612v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17612)). Reinforcement learning did better than imitation in one study of coding agents. SWE-RL reached 41.0% on SWE-bench Verified with a 70B Llama 3, and improved five out-of-domain tasks "whereas a supervised-finetuning baseline even leads to performance degradation on average" ([arXiv 2502.18449v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18449)). Each of these papers tunes an open model, and the two that compare it with a closed model name the frontier of their day: GPT-3.5 for AgentTuning, and GPT-4o for SWE-RL. In our reading, tuning thus buys a smaller model that does one agent's job, not a model better than the frontier.

**All three providers therefore tell builders to prompt and measure first, and to tune only if that fails.** OpenAI's agent guide builds the prototype "with the most capable model for every task to establish a performance baseline" and then swaps in smaller models, and its 34 pages never mention fine-tuning ([OpenAI, A practical guide to building agents](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf)). Its tuning guide says "The prompt engineering process may be all you need", and its SFT guide adds: "If 50 examples have no impact, rethink your task or prompt before adding training data" ([OpenAI, model optimization](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/model-optimization); [OpenAI, SFT](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/supervised-fine-tuning)). Google's advice is to "Start with prompting to find the optimal prompt. Then, move on to fine-tuning (if required)" ([Google, introduction to tuning](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/tuning)). Anthropic's prompting guide assumes success criteria and tests exist first, and notes that "you can sometimes improve latency and cost more easily by choosing a different model" ([Claude, prompt engineering overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/overview)).

Distillation survives only as a section of the SFT guide, where a large model's captured outputs become the training data for a small one ([OpenAI, SFT](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/supervised-fine-tuning)). In TypeScript the `openai` client 7.49.0 still types `client.fineTuning` and the methods `'supervised' | 'dpo' | 'reinforcement'` (`resources/fine-tuning/jobs/jobs.d.ts`), so the types outlive the service.

The two AWS pages disagree, and we did not test a job, but the lifecycle page leaves no Claude model that can be tuned today.

**Google still sells all three methods on its current models, but only on its enterprise platform.** The Gemini API and AI Studio have had no tunable model since Gemini 1.5 Flash-001 was deprecated in May 2025, and Google has "no immediate plans to bring fine-tuning support" ([Gemini API, model tuning](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/model-tuning)). Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform offers SFT on seven Gemini models, preference tuning on two and RLFT on Gemini 3.5 Flash and 3.1 Flash-Lite ([Google, introduction to tuning](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/tuning)). In our reading, Google's RLFT is the closest match to agent training among the three providers. It scores candidate answers and reasoning with up to 16 reward functions of four kinds (string match, a Gemini judge, sandboxed Python and a Cloud Run endpoint), and it needs only "dozens to a few hundred prompts". Google's lists have a lag of their own. Three of the seven SFT models, and both preference-tuning models, are Gemini 2.5 models that Google retired on 20 October 2026 ([Google, model versions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/model-versions)). Outside the three providers, cloud platforms tune other vendors' models. Bedrock's reinforcement fine-tuning, for instance, serves Amazon Nova 2 Lite, OpenAI's gpt-oss-20B and Qwen3 32B ([Bedrock, reinforcement fine-tuning](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/reinforcement-fine-tuning.html)).

## Training is cheap, so the price per token, the data and the base model's lifespan decide what tuning costs

**A tuning run costs tens to hundreds of dollars at November 2026 prices.** Providers bill training by tokens processed, which Google defines as "the total number of tokens in your training dataset, multiplied by your number of epochs" ([Google, pricing](https://cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/pricing)). Google charges $0.01 per 1,000 training tokens for Gemini 3.5 Flash, or $10 per million, for both SFT and RLFT. OpenAI charges $25 per million for GPT-4.1 and $5 for GPT-4.1 mini ([OpenAI, pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). Take 1,000 examples of 2,000 tokens each, trained for three epochs. That run processes 6 million tokens, so it costs $60 on Gemini 3.5 Flash, $150 on GPT-4.1 and $30 on GPT-4.1 mini. Next to an engineer's time, we judge these sums small.

**The price per token decides whether a tuned model saves money, and on OpenAI it rises.** OpenAI's tuned GPT-4.1 costs $3.00 per million input tokens and $12.00 per million output, against $2.00 and $8.00 for the base model, which is 1.5 times as much. Tuned GPT-4.1 mini costs $0.80 and $3.20 against $0.40 and $1.60, twice as much, and its cached input doubles too ([OpenAI, pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). Because every rate doubles, a tuned GPT-4.1 mini saves money only if each request costs less than half as much in tokens priced at base rates. If the output stays the same length, the tuned prompt must be shorter than half the original prompt, minus twice the output's length. Newer models undercut tuned old ones as well: GPT-6 Luna lists $0.10 per million input tokens, an eighth of tuned GPT-4.1 mini's rate (same page). Google prices a tuned Gemini endpoint like its base model on the global endpoint, and at 1.5 times the base price on a regional endpoint ([Google, pricing](https://cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/pricing)).

**Prompt caching shrinks tuning's main saving by up to ten times.** Tuning saves money by removing a long instruction and example block from every request, and caching already discounts a stable block of that kind. Gemini 3.5 Flash charges $1.50 per million input tokens on the global endpoint and $0.15 for cached input (same page). A 4,000-token block of instructions and examples thus costs $0.006 per request uncached and $0.0006 cached. The $60 training run above pays for itself after 10,000 uncached requests, but only after 100,000 requests if the block was cached, before any cache-storage charge. That calculation is ours, and it leaves out data and evals, which cost more than the run. The calculation shows why volume decides the case. Below some tens of thousands of requests per base model, caching the prompt is cheaper than tuning it away.

**The largest cost is the work that has to be redone when the base model retires.** A tuned model lives only as long as its base: OpenAI's tuned models follow "the same shutdown schedule as their underlying base models" ([OpenAI deprecations](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations)). The bases retired in 2026, or due by January 2027, lasted 16 to 30 months, counting from the date in each model's snapshot id or Google's release table. On that count, GPT-4.1 nano lasted 18 months and Gemini 2.5 Flash 16; Claude 3 Haiku lasted 30 on Bedrock, and GPT-4.1 will reach 21 ([Google, model versions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/model-versions); [Bedrock, model lifecycle](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/model-lifecycle-legacy.html)). The providers promise less than these lifetimes. OpenAI gives at least six months' notice before it retires a generally available model, and Google keeps its current Gemini models for at least 12 months after release (same pages). Each retirement means rebuilding the training set for the new base, retraining, and rerunning the evals. The MAP teams named exactly this cost when they called tuning "brittle to model upgrades" ([MAP, arXiv 2512.04123v4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04123)). We infer that the data and the evals are the parts that survive a retirement, so they are what a team should own. OpenAI's own evals dashboard and API turn read-only on 30 November 2026, with Promptfoo named as the replacement ([OpenAI deprecations](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations)).

## Deployed agents rarely tune, and the vendors that do train inside their own harness

**MAP's interviews show prompting as the norm and tuning as the exception, chosen for latency, volume, domain knowledge or cost.** Of the 20 teams the study interviewed, 14 used off-the-shelf models with no SFT or reinforcement learning, and two said the base models already met their needs. Five used SFT. Two of them applied it consistently, "targeting business-specific contexts where domain knowledge improves performance", and three applied it selectively for enterprise clients "where training data is available and cost trade-offs make sense". Four of the five combined a tuned model with off-the-shelf ones. The two domain-knowledge teams are a deployed counterpoint to the knowledge studies above, although MAP reports their choice and not a measured comparison with retrieval. Only one team, which builds a scientific-discovery agent, used a model trained with reinforcement learning, though three more teams planned to ([MAP, arXiv 2512.04123v4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04123), §5.2). Latency changed the picture: three of the four teams with a bounded-latency requirement tuned, which the authors report as "an observation rather than a prevalence claim". One team that used an open model tuned it for a high-volume workload "where inference costs are prohibitive" (§5.1). The teams' stated reason for avoiding tuning was its upkeep, since SFT and RL "require substantial implementation effort and are brittle to model upgrades" (§5.2).

**Rereading MAP at its source changes what its 70% can support.** The abstract places "70% rely on prompting off-the-shelf models instead of weight tuning" beside a survey of 86 deployed systems, so the figure reads as a survey statistic. The body shows it is 14 of the 20 interviewed teams. Those teams were found by "snowball sampling", starting from "agent teams from major technology companies with significant AI research within our professional network" (§3.1). The survey seems not to have asked about tuning at all. The appendix gives survey question ids for 16 of its 17 analysis aspects, but none for "Model selection (open- versus closed, post-training)". That reading is ours. The book plan's fact-check log records the quote as confirmed from the abstract, which it is ([book plan fact-check](../../plans/reviews/fact-check-build-book.md), rows 105 and 174). The book should still say "14 of 20 interviewed teams", not "70% of production agents", and change 1 below proposes that correction.

**The coding-agent vendors that train with reinforcement learning do it in their own harness, at a scale few teams can match.** Cursor trained Composer with reinforcement learning so that it can "call any tool in the Cursor Agent harness". Training ran "hundreds of thousands of concurrent sandboxed coding environments" on "thousands of NVIDIA GPUs", and the result generates code "four times faster than similar models" ([Cursor, Composer](https://cursor.com/blog/composer)). Cognition trained SWE-1.5 with "End-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) on real task environments using our custom Cascade agent harness". It serves the model at up to 950 tokens per second, "13x faster than Sonnet 4.5" ([Cognition, SWE-1.5](https://cognition.ai/blog/swe-1-5)). Both are vendor posts: Cursor measured Composer on its own benchmark, and Cognition on Scale AI's SWE-Bench Pro. Both also concede quality to the frontier: Cursor's footnote says GPT-5 and Sonnet 4.5 "both outperform Composer", and Cognition claims "near-SOTA coding performance". In our reading, what reinforcement learning bought these vendors was speed at near-frontier quality.

**A model trained with reinforcement learning is tied to the harness it was trained in.** Cognition says so directly. SWE-1.5 scored better in its own harness than in others, "a greater incidence of tool call failures" explains part of the gap, and "models tuned for the other harnesses could perform just as well if not better (e.g. Sonnet in Claude Code)". Its loop also shows the cost of that tie: the team "made adjustments to tools and prompts, and then re-trained the model on the updated harness" ([Cognition, SWE-1.5](https://cognition.ai/blog/swe-1-5)). Our harness is designed the other way round, with every component removable "so it can be ablated as models improve" ([AGENTS.md](../../../AGENTS.md#planned-architecture-when-code-starts)). A model tuned to today's tools would make each such change a retraining job, which is our inference from Cognition's account.

## Tune only when four conditions hold, and pick the method by the signal you have

**The evidence supports a rule with four conditions, and the rule is our synthesis rather than any source's.** Tune a model for an agent only when all four hold:
1. an eval of real cases shows a repeated failure of behaviour (a format, a label, a tone, the shape of a tool call), not missing knowledge;
2. better prompts, examples, a different model and a prompt optimiser such as GEPA have each been tried against that eval and fallen short;
3. latency or volume demands a smaller model, and at that volume the saving beats training plus re-tuning, counted against a cached prompt rather than an uncached one;
4. the base model has a published retirement date far enough away to repay the work, and the team owns the training set and the eval, so it can redo the job on the next base.

The first condition follows the provider guides and the knowledge studies in the behaviour section. The second follows Medprompt, many-shot prompting and GEPA. The third follows the price arithmetic and the MAP teams that tuned for latency, and the fourth follows the retirement dates and the teams' complaint about upgrades.

**When all four conditions hold, the training signal on hand decides the method.** Labelled examples of the right output call for SFT, and OpenAI suggests starting with 50 of them ([OpenAI, SFT](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/supervised-fine-tuning)). Pairs of a better and a worse answer call for preference tuning, which suits tone and style ([OpenAI, model optimization](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/model-optimization); [Google, introduction to tuning](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/tuning)). A grader that can score an attempt, such as a test suite or a SQL checker, calls for reinforcement fine-tuning, which Google says needs only "dozens to a few hundred prompts" ([Google, introduction to tuning](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/tuning)). A large model that already passes the eval calls for distillation: log its outputs, keep those that pass, and train a small model on them, as OpenAI's SFT guide describes ([OpenAI, SFT](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/supervised-fine-tuning)). OpenAI calls logging a pilot's prompts and outputs for this purpose "prompt baking" ([OpenAI, optimizing LLM accuracy](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/optimizing-llm-accuracy)). Whatever the method, the tuned model then goes through the full eval again, safety cases included, because tuning can weaken what the provider trained in.

## In TypeScript a tuned model is just a new model id, and our gateway prices it only from a catalog entry

**None of our eight SDKs trains a model, so the book's TypeScript story starts where the training ends.** A tuning job runs in a provider's console or API, and the agent then calls the result by its id, which OpenAI writes as `ft:gpt-4.1-nano-2025-04-14:openai::BTz2REMH` in its own example ([OpenAI, SFT](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/supervised-fine-tuning)). The one TypeScript surface for training that we found is a provider's REST client: `openai` 7.49.0, installed beside `@openai/agents` 0.21.0, types `client.fineTuning` and three job methods ([repo notes](notes/our-repo.md)). We did not survey open-source TypeScript training libraries, and the papers above all train in Python-based stacks or in provider services, so TypeScript sources on training itself are thin.

**Our gateway treats a tuned model as unknown until a caller gives it a price.** `createModel()` looks the model up by provider and exact id with `findCatalogEntry()`, and the shipped catalog lists four base models (three Claude 5.5 models and `gpt-5.4-mini`), none of them on a provider's tuning list. A model the catalog lacks "has an unknown price, never a guessed zero", and without catalog limits it falls back to the adapter's default caps or throws `missing_caps` (`packages/gateway/src/cost.ts`, `create-model.ts`, `catalog.ts`). The behaviour is correct for tuned models, because their rates differ from the base: twice the base rate for GPT-4.1 mini, and 1.5 times on a regional Gemini endpoint. A catalog that a caller passes replaces the shipped one instead of adding to it (the `catalog` option in `runtime-types.ts`). A caller who runs a tuned model therefore passes a whole catalog: an entry for the tuned model, with its tuned rates, `asOf` date and source page, and an entry for every other model it uses. A billed total from the provider would win over the catalog (`foldCost()` in `cost.ts`), but neither shipped adapter reports one, since both set the cost basis to `unknown` (`adapters/wire.ts`). The book can show this as the practical meaning of "a tuned model is a new model".

## Chapter T11's section should teach four points, and stays a section

**The research fits one section of Chapter T11, so T39's risk of needing a chapter does not arise.** The topic answers one question a reader meets while choosing a model, and the chapter's model-choice section already teaches the first step: start with the most capable model, then try smaller ones against evals ([T11](../../todos/done/T11-chapter-talking-to-a-model.md)). T39's section should teach four points, in this order:
1. **Tuning changes how a model behaves more than what it knows.** In controlled studies retrieval beats tuning for facts, and tuning on new facts raises hallucination, although two MAP teams tuned for domain knowledge. For formats, labels and tone, tuning works (Ovadia et al.; Gekhman et al.; OpenAI's matrix; MAP).
2. **Prompt and measure first, and tune to move one job to a smaller model.** All three providers put prompting and evals first. Careful prompting beat tuned specialists in medicine, and in our reading the agent-tuning papers buy a small model that does one agent's job (Medprompt; GEPA; FireAct; SWE-RL).
3. **A tuned model is a new model with its own price and a short life.** It costs up to twice the base rate per token on OpenAI, it retires with its base model 16 to 30 months after that model's release, and, we conclude, it needs the safety evals of a new model (OpenAI pricing and deprecations; Google model versions; Qi et al.).
4. **Deployed agents mostly prompt.** In MAP, 14 of 20 interviewed teams prompted off-the-shelf models, and the teams that tuned gave latency, volume, domain knowledge or cost as their reasons. The vendors that train agent models with reinforcement learning tie them to their own harness (MAP; Cursor; Cognition).

**Two facts in the section will age fastest, and every vendor result needs a label.** The provider status of November 2026 (OpenAI closing, Anthropic absent, Google alone) should sit in one dated note, so that a later edition changes one place. The prices should carry their date as well. Results from Predibase, Anthropic, Cursor and Cognition describe their own products, and the section should name them as vendor results, as this report does.

**T39's analogy works, and it breaks in a second place besides the one T39 names.** Sending an employee on a year-long course, against briefing a skilled contractor, captures the up-front cost of tuning. T39 notes that a tuned model still needs the brief. We propose a second break, drawn from the retirement dates: the course's certificate expires when the base model retires, and the employee must then retake the course. Change 2 below proposes adding it.

## One offline example would give the section its listing, and no example should call a tuning API

**The section needs one listing, and the best fit prices a tuned model against a cached prompt.** The example would run offline in `verify/vercel-ai-sdk/examples/`, where T28 already prices a streamed call from its usage ([T28](../../todos/done/T28-example-streaming-usage.md)). It would script two mock calls. The first sends a 4,000-token prefix of instructions and examples plus a 200-token question to a base model, and the second sends the question alone to a tuned model id. Both answer in 300 tokens. A small price table holds GPT-4.1 mini's base and tuned rates, dated November 2026. With those numbers the base call costs $0.00216 uncached and $0.00096 with the prefix cached, while the tuned call costs $0.00112. The example asserts four behaviours with `check()`:
1. the tuned call pays twice the base rate per token;
2. the uncached base call costs more than the tuned call;
3. the cached base call costs less than the tuned call;
4. against the uncached prompt, a $30 training run breaks even after 28,847 requests, and against the cached prompt it never does.

It uses no network, no account and no training job, so it passes the repo's offline test rule. The book should leave out any listing that creates a real tuning job, for three reasons. First, it needs a network and a provider account. Second, OpenAI no longer accepts new organisations. Third, we judge that its output would date the book faster than any other listing.

## Prompt, retrieve and measure, and tune only what the eval proves

The research question asked when fine-tuning, distillation or reinforcement fine-tuning beats prompting plus tools for an agent, what it costs, and what deployed agents show. Tuning beats prompting for behaviour more than for knowledge, and mostly when a smaller model must do one job at a latency, volume or cost that a frontier model cannot meet. Training costs tens to hundreds of dollars, and in our judgement the per-token premium, the data, the evals and the re-tuning at each base-model retirement are the larger costs. Deployed agents mostly prompt: 14 of 20 interviewed teams in MAP used no tuning, and the vendors that train agent models with reinforcement learning tie them to their own harness. As of November 2026, OpenAI is closing its tuning platform, Anthropic offers none in its own API, and Google alone tunes its current models. The book should therefore teach readers to prompt, retrieve and measure first, and to tune only when an eval proves a gap that will outlive the base model. Our own SDK and harness should stay built for that order, and since the owner approved change 6 on 2026-11-19, the SDK plan says so.
