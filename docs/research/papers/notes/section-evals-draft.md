## A trustworthy eval suite needs its own fresh tasks and a baseline agent, because the published benchmarks grade too loosely to gate a release

The four general-purpose agent benchmarks that most shaped 2024-era evaluation practice share one weakness: each grades success in a way a weak or lucky agent can satisfy without doing the work the benchmark claims to measure. GAIA pairs 466 real-world assistant questions, which need reasoning, web browsing, tool use and multiple modalities, with a 92% human pass rate against 15% for GPT-4 with plugins, showing the gap a general assistant must close [verified against the abstract] (Mialon et al., [arXiv:2311.12983](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12983), submitted 21 November 2023). SWE-bench asks a model to resolve a real GitHub issue by producing a patch that a hidden test suite accepts: FAIL_TO_PASS tests must flip from failing to passing and PASS_TO_PASS tests must keep passing, across 2,294 Python-repository instances (Jimenez et al., [arXiv:2310.06770](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770), submitted 10 October 2023; grading method per [swebench.com](https://www.swebench.com/SWE-bench/guides/evaluation/)). AgentBench instead spans eight interactive environments, from an operating system shell to web shopping, and found commercial models far ahead of open models up to 70B parameters, with most failures traced to weak long-term reasoning and poor instruction-following rather than any single skill gap (Liu et al., [arXiv:2308.03688](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03688), submitted 7 August 2023; ICLR 2024). τ-bench, finally, drops a single-turn task for a simulated conversation: a language-model user, equipped with a persona, talks to an agent that must follow written domain policy while calling real API tools, and a run counts as a pass only when the database ends in the stated goal state (Yao, Shinn, Razavi and Narasimhan, [arXiv:2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045), submitted 17 June 2024). Its pass^k metric, which repeats each task and asks whether an agent succeeds on every repetition, showed gpt-4o-era agents clearing under half of tasks once, and under a quarter eight times in a row on the retail domain. τ²-bench then added a dual-control telecom domain where the user, not only the agent, must act on shared state through tools, and found large score drops once the user could act too (Barres, Dong, Ray, Si and Narasimhan, [arXiv:2506.07982](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07982), submitted 9 June 2025).

Both coding and policy-following benchmarks turned out to reward passing their own grader rather than the behavior they were built to test. OpenAI's human-filtered SWE-bench Verified, a 500-task subset that 93 Python-experienced developers triple-screened for clear descriptions, correct test patches and solvability, raised GPT-4o's resolve rate to 33.2% at launch and became the field's default coding leaderboard (announced 13 August 2024; curation and task count confirmed directly on the benchmark's own site, [swebench.com/verified](https://swebench.com/verified); OpenAI's original announcement page returned an HTTP error to our fetch tool, so its wording is reconstructed from a search summary [secondary]). By February 2026, OpenAI said it would stop reporting scores on it, citing rising contamination because the underlying GitHub repositories sit inside most providers' training data [secondary: OpenAI's explanatory post also returned an HTTP error to our fetch tool, so this claim rests on a search-result paraphrase, not a primary read]. "The SWE-Bench Illusion" gives that concern a number: asked only for a bug's file path, with no repository in context, state-of-the-art models reach up to 76% accuracy on SWE-bench tasks, but only up to 53% on tasks drawn from repositories SWE-bench never included, a gap the authors read as memorization rather than localization skill (Liang, Garg and Zilouchian Moghaddam, [arXiv:2506.12286](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12286), submitted 14 June 2025). The Agentic Benchmark Checklist, a 25-author audit of common agentic-benchmark flaws, names a second and independent problem: SWE-bench Verified's test suites are too thin to prove a patch is actually correct, and τ-bench's own grader counts an empty response as a pass, letting a trivial agent clear 38% of its tasks (Zhu et al., [arXiv:2507.02825](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02825), submitted 3 July 2025). Applying their checklist to CVE-Bench cut the benchmark's overestimate of agent performance by 33%. We found no comparably direct, source-backed critique naming GAIA or AgentBench as flawed, so we say plainly that the contamination-and-weak-grading pattern is best documented for coding and tool-use benchmarks, not confirmed across every general-purpose one. Browser- and web-specific benchmarks such as WebArena and OSWorld face a parallel measurement problem, covered already in our browser-use report ([browser-use.md](../../browser-use/browser-use.md#only-self-hosted-deterministically-graded-suites-can-gate-a-release)).

Two papers from the same week in November 2026 sharpen two pieces of this picture: what a coding-agent eval should cost to run, and what an eval needs before it can run at all. "Reducing Inference Cost of Coding Agents with Timeout Cascades" evaluates 36 open-weight models released since 2024 on SWE-bench Verified and finds that small models now close most of the accuracy gap with large ones on easy and medium tasks, leaving the gap concentrated on hard ones (Zhou, Sviridov, Ustiugov and Javaid, [arXiv:2611.24324](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24324), submitted 18 November 2026). Their proposed cascade tries a small model first and escalates to a larger one only on timeout, cutting total GPU-hour cost by up to 33% in their tested regime and 16.4% across all tasks, while nearly matching an oracle router that always picks the right model size. Because the paper measures cost and accuracy on SWE-bench Verified, its findings inherit that benchmark's contamination caveats above; a cascade result on a cleaner suite would carry more weight. "EnvAgent" addresses the step before any coding task runs: building the Docker environment a repository needs, which today means brittle hand-written Dockerfiles that scale poorly and can bias whatever eval depends on them (Cheng, Low, Huang, Zhang, Chen, Keahey, Marshall and Pei, [arXiv:2611.23258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23258), submitted 1 October 2026; accepted at the AI4S workshop at SC26). EnvAgent pairs deterministic Docker tools with coordinated agents for repository analysis, Dockerfile synthesis and iterative repair, and the paper introduces ENVBENCH, a 54-repository, seven-language suite with a declarative rubric and a shared evaluator that applies 2,108 weighted checks per environment. Under matched model settings, EnvAgent built 43.5 of 54 environments to a working state, against 23.5 for Codex and Claude Code, and scored 62.02 on the composite executability-and-rubric metric against their 24.19. ENVBENCH is therefore less a rival to SWE-bench than a benchmark for the setup step our own sandbox conformance suite and verification harnesses also depend on getting right.

Three conclusions follow for a harness builder choosing what an eval suite should rely on. First, a published leaderboard number is not evidence of capability by itself; the grading method behind it, FAIL_TO_PASS tests, a database goal state, or a rubric check, decides whether a pass means what it claims, so any suite we adopt needs its grader read as closely as its task list. Second, the tasks most exposed to contamination are the oldest and most reused, so a suite that stays trustworthy needs either fresh tasks the field has not trained on or a design, like ENVBENCH's weighted rubric checks, that a memorized answer cannot satisfy on its own. Third, cost and correctness trade off in measurable ways, as the timeout-cascade paper shows, so a mature suite should report cost alongside pass rate rather than pass rate alone.

This points directly at our own eval plan: [N38](../../../todos/ready/N38-seed-suite-baseline.md)'s seed suite already runs each task on both our harness and mini-SWE-agent, the roughly 100-line bash-only agent that SWE-bench's own "Bash Only" leaderboard uses as its baseline, which gives us a second grader's worth of protection against any single benchmark's grading flaws; [N36](../../../todos/ready/N36-eval-reports-replay.md), [N37](../../../todos/ready/N37-inspect-bridge.md) and [N39](../../../todos/ready/N39-ablation-runs.md) build the reporting, replay and ablation machinery around it, while [A17](../../../todos/ready/A17-eval-tasks-graders.md) and [W12](../../../todos/ready/W12-coding-agent-eval-tasks.md) still need their own tasks written with FAIL_TO_PASS-style tests or rubric checks, not single-string-match grading, if they are to survive the scrutiny SWE-bench Verified did not.
