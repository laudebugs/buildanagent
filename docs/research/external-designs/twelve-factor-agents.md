# 12-factor agents confirms our core design and exposes one gap: ingress

- **Status:** research report for todo U9 ("Round U" research), 2026-11-18. It reads an external design, HumanLayer's 12-factor agents, against our guides, plans and decisions, and it closes a gap that our [OSS-frameworks notes](../notes/oss-frameworks.md) left open (see [Later closures](../fact-check-2026-11-18.md#later-closures)).
- **Versions checked (as of November 2026):** `humanlayer/12-factor-agents` at commit [`d20c728`](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/tree/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc) (2025-09-21), the tip of `main` on 2026-11-18; our repo at `main` commit `e7c79b1` (2026-11-18). npm versions are the registry's `latest` on 2026-11-18.
- **Evidence:** two research notes, [the essay from its source](notes/twelve-factor-agents-source.md) and [the factors mapped onto our design](notes/factor-mapping-to-our-design.md). They cite the repo at that commit and its full git history, the npm registry, humanlayer.dev and this repo's guides, plans, decisions and research, all read on 2026-11-18. Corrections are logged in [fact-check-twelve-factor-agents.md](fact-check-twelve-factor-agents.md).
- **Verified:** every factor title and line reference, date, commit, licence, package version and quotation was re-read against its primary source on 2026-11-18, and so was every claim about our own documents. Nothing in the external repo was run. Its contents, including an agent-instruction `CLAUDE.md`, were read as data and not followed. The essay is CC BY-SA 4.0, so this report quotes it briefly, with attribution, and does not mirror it.
- **Our proposal, not sourced:** the five plan changes in the last section (an ingress design, write identities and outcomes, two error-handling defaults, pre-fetching, and an internal-question pause), and the three items we keep out.

Most agent frameworks hide the loop, the prompt and the pause points, and our plans rest on the opposite bet: write the thin parts ourselves. HumanLayer's 12-factor agents is the best-known practitioner statement of that bet. Our earlier framework research named it a canonical source for the no-framework argument but could not fetch it. The essay asks builders to own the loop, the prompt, the context and the state, to let the model propose a structured next step, and to let deterministic code decide whether to run it, pause for a person, or stop ([README](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/README.md)). In this report, I check the essay's twelve factors and its appendix "Factor 13" against our guides and plans, weigh its evidence, and show where our own measured research supports or qualifies it.

Measured against our documents, we align with **8 of its 13 factors** (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12) and partly with 4 more (3, 5, 7, 13). The essay brings no measurements of its own, and it comes from a vendor whose original product sold the pause, human-contact and trigger features that three factors call for. Our research supplies the missing numbers: they back small agents, owned control flow and errors returned as data. The same research qualifies the essay in three places. Context engineering pays less as windows grow, a forked thread cannot undo writes to the helpdesk, and fast human approval decays into rubber-stamping. **12-factor agents confirms the core of our design, and its one real lesson for us is factor 11: we have no design for how ticket and approval events reach the agent.**

## One design hides behind twelve uneven factors

**The essay reduces an agent to four parts, and its factors elaborate them unevenly.** It opens with a short history of software. Flowcharts gave way to DAG orchestrators such as Airflow and Prefect, and agents promised that "you get to throw the DAG away" ([brief-history L36](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/brief-history-of-software.md)). That promise breaks on one problem: "Agents get lost when the context window gets too long… literally thats it, but that's enough to kneecap the approach" (L91-92). The author's alternative is the "micro agent", a short LLM loop inside a mostly deterministic workflow. In his deploybot example, code deploys to staging and runs the end-to-end tests. It then hands the agent one task, "deploy SHA 4af9ec0 to production", inside a workflow of 5-10 steps where a person approves each deploy (L118-155). An agent, in this view, is **a prompt, a switch statement, accumulated context, and a for loop** that runs until a terminal tool call (L161-164).

**Read together, factors 3, 5, 8 and 12 describe a single design.** A typed, append-only event list holds the whole state. A custom serializer renders that list into the prompt as XML-style tagged events ([factor 3 L120-140](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-03-own-your-context-window.md)). A hand-written switch then either runs each proposed intent and continues, or saves the thread and breaks out to wait for a person or a webhook ([factor 8 L27-69](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-08-own-your-control-flow.md)). Factor 13, an appendix, adds one rule to that design: fetch likely context in code instead of spending model round trips on it. In the author's words, "If you already know what tools you'll want the model to call, just call them DETERMINISTICALLY" ([appendix 13 L147](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/appendix-13-pre-fetch.md)).

**The other factors vary from concrete guidance to a joke.** Factors 3, 7, 8 and 9 carry the most concrete guidance. Factors 1, 4 and 12 are framings rather than practices; factor 4, for example, says tools are "just structured output from your LLM that triggers deterministic code" ([factor 4 L5](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-04-tools-are-structured-outputs.md)). Factors 6 and 11 are product requirements with no code. Factor 12, "Make your agent a stateless reducer", makes no argument at all. Its only prose is "This one is mostly just for fun", followed by two images, one of them a tweet that calls an agent "a foldL where the accumulator is a context window" ([factor 12](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-12-stateless-reducer.md)). The essay's code snippets are illustrative pseudocode that mixes Python and JavaScript syntax, so this report marks none of them as verified.

**The sharpest technical claim is a durable pause between choosing a tool and running it.** The author's "number one feature request" for every framework is the ability to interrupt "between the moment of tool **selection** and the moment of tool **invocation**". Without it, a builder must pause in memory and restart from scratch after a crash, restrict the agent to low-stakes calls, or "yolo hope it doesn't screw up" ([factor 8 L73-81](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-08-own-your-control-flow.md)). The repo's TypeScript template turns this claim into code. Its `agentLoop` returns the thread on `done_for_now`, `request_more_information`, `request_approval_from_manager` or the risky `divide` tool. An HTTP webhook later re-enters the loop with the person's answer appended as an event ([template src/agent.ts](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/packages/create-12-factor-agent/template/src/agent.ts); [src/server.ts L58-120](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/packages/create-12-factor-agent/template/src/server.ts)).

## The evidence is one vendor's experience, and it carries no measurements

**No factor is backed by data.** The framework thesis rests on a self-reported journey. Builders reach a "70-80% quality bar", find that getting past 80% "requires reverse-engineering the framework", and "Start over from scratch" ([README, "Why 12-factor agents?"](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/README.md)). Neither the author's "at least 100 SaaS builders" nor the 70-80% figure comes with a method. The central causal claim, that long context degrades agents, rests on intuition and a quoted tweet. Factor 10's band of "3-10, maybe 20 steps max" has no step-count data behind it ([factor 10 L9](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-10-small-focused-agents.md)). Factor 3, the longest factor, offers one screenshot of a message in fewer tokens, plus tweets showing that the term "context engineering" caught on. It never compares its custom format with standard messages on task success ([factor 3 L240-253](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-03-own-your-context-window.md)). Even factor 7, which tells the model to always emit JSON, hedges: "you might not get any performance boost from this, but you should experiment" ([factor 7 L19](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-07-contact-humans-with-tools.md)).

**The conflicts of interest are open, and they tilt the essay toward human approval.** Factor 11 opens, "If you're waiting for the humanlayer pitch, you made it" ([factor 11 L5](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-11-trigger-from-anywhere.md)). Pause and resume, human contact as tool calls, and multi-channel triggers (factors 6, 7 and 11) are the problems that HumanLayer's original approvals API sold a solution to. The template depends on `humanlayer` `^0.7.7` and an unpinned `@boundaryml/baml` ([template package.json](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/packages/create-12-factor-agent/template/package.json)). Factor 2's prompt example and the template's prompts are written in BAML, from BoundaryML, and the README says the author runs webinars with @hellovai ([README, Related Resources](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/README.md)). Hacker News readers see the same pattern in the author's later work. On a 2026 post, one commenter called it "just a long ad for his product" ([HN 49033041](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49033041), secondary and hostile). On a 2025 post, another said that, the product push aside, its basics on compaction and human review "are pretty good" ([HN 45350892](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45350892), secondary). Because most of the advice amounts to "do it yourself", the bias shifts emphasis more than substance. It does inflate the value of fast human loop-in, the one claim our evidence contradicts.

**The essay and its code are now historical.** The repo was first committed on 2025-03-30, and the essay was published in early April 2025, a date inferred from a dated screenshot and from factor 3's "About 2 months after" remark. Dex Horthy wrote 248 of its 273 commits. Its last substantive commit, `52ae9f9` on 2025-09-21, is titled "there will be no v1.1", and it removed the README's pointer to a v1.1 draft ([repo at the pinned commit](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/tree/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc)). No commit has landed on `main` since that day.

**The template no longer installs as written.** The `create-12-factor-agent` package that the README invites people to help build does not exist on npm ([registry.npmjs.org/create-12-factor-agent](https://registry.npmjs.org/create-12-factor-agent)). The template's `^0.7.7` range still resolves to `humanlayer` 0.7.7, the April 2025 TypeScript client. Since 0.8.0, though, that npm name has shipped a command-line tool, and it stands at `0.17.2-npm` as of November 2026 ([registry.npmjs.org/humanlayer](https://registry.npmjs.org/humanlayer)). The template's server also imports `@humanlayer/sdk`, which its `package.json` never declares and which was last released as 0.7.9 on 2025-06-03 ([registry.npmjs.org/@humanlayer/sdk](https://registry.npmjs.org/@humanlayer/sdk)). HumanLayer itself has moved from agents that contact humans to "a multiplayer coding agent IDE and cloud" ([humanlayer.dev](https://www.humanlayer.dev/), fetched 2026-11-18). The template is therefore a historical artefact, not a starter to install.

**The licence allows short quotes but not a mirror.** The essay's prose and images are licensed CC BY-SA 4.0 and its code Apache-2.0, yet the `LICENSE` file holds only the Apache text ([README, License](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/README.md)). Short attributed quotes are fine. Mirroring the prose into `docs/reference/` would carry the share-alike terms with it (our reading, not legal advice).

**The essay is popular, but our corpus engages only with the author's later work.** A shields.io badge reports about 27k GitHub stars as of November 2026, a figure we could not check against GitHub itself ([shields.io](https://img.shields.io/github/stars/humanlayer/12-factor-agents.json), secondary). Our Hacker News corpus never names "12-factor", although it holds 517- and 394-point stories by the same author on later context-engineering topics ([HN 45347532](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45347532); [HN 49023019](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49023019)). The essay's claim to have started the "context engineering" trend is a self-claim ([factor 3 L247](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-03-own-your-context-window.md)). Its value to us is a clear practitioner statement of the no-framework argument, not new evidence.

**Factors 4, 6 and 8 form the core of our design, and we go further than the essay.** Its "number one feature request", a durable pause between tool selection and invocation, sits at the centre of SDK decision 8, harness decision HD5 and agent decision AD1. In our SDK, an approval ends the run in a signed, resumable log state rather than blocking a callback. The harness writes `approval_requested` before it asks any client, signs every pause with `hmacSigner()` over a persistent key ring, and re-runs its permission pipeline on resume ([HD5](../../plans/build-harness.md#hd5-the-harness-always-passes-hmacsigner-over-a-persistent-key-ring-accepted-with-sdk-d5)). The essay's three bad options match what our SDK survey found. Claude and Pi pauses are blocking callbacks that die with the process, and Codex calls its approval requests "Transient, non-durable events" ([SDK guide §7](../../guides/agent-sdk.md#7-an-approval-should-end-the-run-in-a-signed-resumable-log-state-not-block-a-callback); [harness guide §2](../../guides/agent-harness.md#2-the-session-log-should-be-one-append-only-tree-of-typed-entries-with-head-a-writer-lock-and-pauses-as-entries)). Our nearest match to the essay's "Multiple Human Access" is a role-based N-of-M quorum. We built it because "Every approval API we surveyed returns one decision" ([AD3](../../plans/build-agent.md#ad3-a-role-based-quorum-per-tier-and-amount-band-bound-to-the-host-signed-pause-and-expiring-on-age-or-policy-change-accepted-with-sdk-d5-and-hd5); [agent guide §1](../../guides/agent.md#1-the-agent-owns-the-product-decisions-and-the-sdk-and-harness-own-the-machinery-beneath-them)).

**Practitioner counts back factors 2 and 8, the essay's case for owning prompts and loops.** In our Hacker News synthesis, about 185 authors across 101 threads hold that "a thin loop you can read beats a framework", and LangChain draws about 46 authors, "almost all negative" ([practitioner research §1](../practitioners/practitioner-research.md#1-eleven-views-have-wide-support-led-by-quota-opacity-sandboxed-autonomy-and-plan-files)). A dissent deserves weight, though. About 25 authors say the thin loop's missing 10% is the real work: error handling, timeouts, context management and UI ([SDK guide §9](../../guides/agent-sdk.md#9-the-public-api-should-be-functions-with-options-objects-an-explicit-provider-and-nothing-that-reaches-outside-the-process-by-default)). Our SDK guide answers both views with one deliverable: a full loop beside a documented reference loop of a few hundred lines (same section).

**Benchmarks back factor 10, small agents inside deterministic code.** On τ-bench, the gpt-4o retail agent fell from **0.604 at pass^1 to 0.383 at pass^4**, so a policy that must never break belongs in code, not in a longer loop ([agent guide §2](../../guides/agent.md#2-choose-the-least-autonomy-the-task-allows-a-workflow-in-code-with-one-bounded-loop-for-the-ambiguous-middle)). The essay's band of "3-10, maybe 20" steps brackets our role caps of 2, 8 and 2 and our SDK default of 20 model calls. Its advice to grow agent scope slowly as models improve matches our removability rule, and that rule rests on a measured trend. OpenHands' gain over mini-SWE-agent fell **from +13.60 to +5.47 points** between Claude 3.7 Sonnet and Claude 4 Sonnet ([harness guide §11](../../guides/agent-harness.md#11-every-component-should-be-removable-because-the-value-of-harness-scaffolding-decays-as-models-improve)). The practitioner support for small tasks comes from coding agents, so it transfers to a support product only weakly ([agent guide §2](../../guides/agent.md#2-choose-the-least-autonomy-the-task-allows-a-workflow-in-code-with-one-bounded-loop-for-the-ambiguous-middle)).

**Our research backs factor 9 and adds a failure mode that the essay misses.** The MCP specification warns that without error results "the LLM would not be able to see that an error occurred and self-correct" ([SDK guide §4](../../guides/agent-sdk.md#4-tools-should-take-standard-schema-validate-inside-the-sdk-and-return-every-failure-to-the-model-as-data)). The essay frames errors as "self-healing", but practitioners report agents that answer a failed tool by inventing a mock. Our default error text therefore tells the model not to substitute one (same section). Our escalation is also deterministic. AD9 sends every `no_progress` stop to a person and never retries ([AD9](../../plans/build-agent.md#ad9-one-budget-per-ticket-across-the-three-roles-and-every-budget-stop-hands-the-ticket-to-a-person-accepted)), which matches the essay's alternative of a "deterministic takeover of the control flow" ([factor 9 L62](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-09-compact-errors.md)).

**Factor 12 holds, but our reducer replays per run, not per step.** `Session.toContext()` rebuilds the `Context` from log entries, and `playback()` makes no model or tool calls ([build-harness §3.2](../../plans/build-harness.md#32-session-log-api)). An in-process crash, however, "loses the turn", and step-level durability waits for a later `execution: "activity"` adapter ([SDK guide §3](../../guides/agent-sdk.md#3-the-loop-should-be-one-in-process-pure-function-capped-in-model-calls-that-ends-with-a-stop-reason); [build-sdk §3.3](../../plans/build-sdk.md#33-sdk-api)). Per-run replay is the right order for a v1, and our plans should say so plainly.

## Our departures share one reason the essay never weighs: external effects

**On factor 3 we keep standard messages, because changing the prefix breaks the prompt cache.** The essay suggests packing history into one user message of XML-style events. Our harness guide treats every prefix change as a cache rebuild. Cloudflare's Think, for example, moves its truncation cutoff only every 8 messages, and our gateway freezes the system prompt until compaction ([harness guide §3](../../guides/agent-harness.md#3-context-policy-should-run-five-removable-layers-cheapest-first-and-keep-every-compaction-visible-and-reversible)). We already deliver most of what the essay says a custom format buys. What we store stays separate from what we pass: compaction never deletes originals, and masking runs "at read time … never in the log" (same section). The essay's "Safety" benefit, filtering sensitive data, is our context split, in which the planner never sees the raw ticket body ([agent guide §5](../../guides/agent.md#5-safety-is-a-policy-table-and-a-context-split-tier-every-tool-by-worst-case-effect-bind-identity-and-cut-the-outbound-leg)). That split holds, because the planner's `support_get_ticket` returns only metadata: subject, queue and attachment names, no body ([A4](../../todos/ready/A4-read-internal-tools.md)).

**Our evidence also shrinks the essay's promise that owning context "can dramatically improve" an agent.** Averaged over four models, the context-management gain on SWE-bench Verified fell **from 35.7 to 2.7 points** as the window grew from 32K to 128K tokens ([harness guide §3](../../guides/agent-harness.md#3-context-policy-should-run-five-removable-layers-cheapest-first-and-keep-every-compaction-visible-and-reversible)). Owning the context still matters. Its payoff, however, is largest for small windows, and neither source measures a custom format against messages.

**On factor 5 we unify execution state and keep business state where it lives.** Our log is "the only source of truth", and "no separate run-state blob exists" ([harness guide §2](../../guides/agent-harness.md#2-the-session-log-should-be-one-append-only-tree-of-typed-entries-with-head-a-writer-lock-and-pauses-as-entries)). OpenAI had to add bookkeeping to `RunState` 1.20 because history and paused-run state live in two stores that can diverge. Our harness research calls that failure "the strongest argument for one store" (same section). The essay's own carve-out for "session ids" and "password contexts" ([factor 5 L28](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-05-unify-execution-state.md)) matches our `TrustedContext`, which is "never in a model-facing schema or the log" ([build-agent §3.2](../../plans/build-agent.md#32-trusted-run-context-helpdesk-and-tool-contracts)).

**The essay errs where it sells forking as a pure benefit.** It praises forking by "copying some subset of the thread" ([factor 5 L37](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-05-unify-execution-state.md)). Our U7 research found the reverse for business effects: **rewind changes what the model sees next; it never reverses a write to someone else's system** ([rewind-vs-rollback](../reversible-actions/rewind-vs-rollback.md)). Worse, after a revert or a forgotten turn, "the model loses sight of effects that still exist and is more likely to repeat them" ([§ "Two ledgers"](../reversible-actions/rewind-vs-rollback.md#two-ledgers-and-the-first-fields-to-add)). Business records live in the helpdesk, which the agent reaches only through one `Helpdesk` interface ([AD14](../../plans/build-agent.md#ad14-one-helpdesk-interface-is-the-agents-only-way-out-with-a-fake-for-tests-a-shadow-wrapper-for-rollout-and-a-real-adapter-the-owner-names-accepted)). Cross-run counters and the kill switch live in a policy store, outside every session log ([AD4](../../plans/build-agent.md#ad4-an-in-process-policy-snapshot-read-synchronously-by-a-permission-hook-with-a-measured-kill-switch-delay-provisional)). A single thread therefore cannot own state that must outlive a fork.

**On factors 7 and 11 our evidence contradicts the essay's case for fast human approval.** The essay argues that quickly looping in humans lets agents take on "higher stakes operations" such as external emails and production writes ([factor 11 L15](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-11-trigger-from-anywhere.md)). Practitioners report the opposite trend. About 440 authors run without prompts inside a sandbox, because "any security mechanism which relies on users to read and intelligently respond to approval prompts is doomed to fail over time" ([harness guide §5](../../guides/agent-harness.md#5-the-permission-pipeline-should-be-ordered-deny-first-and-fail-closed-and-its-approvals-should-be-signed-entries)). The GitHub MCP exploit got through partly because "many users already opt for an 'Always Allow' confirmation policy" ([agent guide §5](../../guides/agent.md#5-safety-is-a-policy-table-and-a-context-split-tier-every-tool-by-worst-case-effect-bind-identity-and-cut-the-outbound-leg)). We therefore tier tools by worst-case effect, so that prompts stay a scarce budget.

**For the same reason, our model never contacts a customer directly.** Code turns a `missing` fact into a templated question, and a person approves it through the T3 reply gate, because a question to a customer is external communication ([AD12](../../plans/build-agent.md#ad12-a-missing-fact-becomes-a-templated-question-through-the-reply-gate-and-missing-authority-becomes-a-handoff-provisional)). Factor 7's underlying instinct, to ask instead of guess, still has wide support. About 60 Hacker News authors across 53 threads wish their agent had asked ([SDK guide §7](../../guides/agent-sdk.md#7-an-approval-should-end-the-run-in-a-signed-resumable-log-state-not-block-a-callback)).

**We also decline two smaller ideas: a run-time LLM judge and role hacking.** Factor 8 lists "LLM-as-judge on structured output" among custom loop hooks ([factor 8 L12-17](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-08-own-your-control-flow.md)). We use a judge only in evals and rely on code checks at run time, such as requiring `orderIds` to be a subset of the requester's orders. AD10 rules that no classifier approves any call and that a classifier may only raise a tier ([AD10](../../plans/build-agent.md#ad10-no-classifier-approves-any-call-and-a-classifier-may-only-raise-a-tier)). Factor 2's "role hacking" needs nonstandard roles, but both of our first wire protocols are chat APIs, and nothing measures a gain ([build-sdk D2](../../plans/build-sdk.md#d2-anthropic-messages-and-openai-compatible-chat-completions-first-accepted)).

## Five small changes follow, led by an ingress design

The following recommendations are ours; neither the essay nor our research prescribes them. Each can be tested offline, and none reverses an accepted decision.

**First, design ingress, the largest gap.** The agent plan receives an `InboundTicket { ticketId, channel, authToken }` "as the helpdesk delivers it" and replies through `Helpdesk.sendReply` ([build-agent §3.2](../../plans/build-agent.md#32-trusted-run-context-helpdesk-and-tool-contracts)). No document says how a ticket event or an approval reaches `triage()` or `resolve()`. A search of the agent plan and its todos for "webhook", "cron" and "idempot" finds nothing (our grep, 2026-11-18). The harness plan defers a network server, which "waits for a client that needs one" ([build-harness §1](../../plans/build-harness.md#1-goal-and-non-goals)), and the triage agent is that client. Add an ingress component, either inside A24's scope ([A24](../../todos/ready/A24-real-helpdesk-adapter.md)) or as a new Stage 5 todo. The component is a webhook receiver or poller that turns helpdesk events into `triage(InboundTicket)` calls and approval-surface callbacks into `resolve()` calls. It must survive at-least-once delivery: deduplicate on the helpdesk event id, and allow one `triage()` per ticket version. The session writer lock returns `"would_block"` for concurrent writers ([HD2](../../plans/build-harness.md#hd2-a-lock-file-with-a-renewed-lease-on-local-disk-only-provisional)), but it cannot stop a re-triage after a run completes. Record the design as AD15 or an ADR, and test it with `fakeHelpdesk` delivering duplicate and out-of-order events.

**Second, give every helpdesk write an identity and an outcome.** This change is factor 5 on our terms. Execution state stays unified in the log, and the log records the identity of each business effect without claiming to own it. U7's recommendation applies directly: "Give each write an identity before it runs and an outcome after it runs, and treat the gap between the two as `unknown`" ([rewind-vs-rollback](../reversible-actions/rewind-vs-rollback.md#two-ledgers-and-the-first-fields-to-add)). Concretely, add an idempotency key, written before the call, and an outcome of `applied | not_applied | unknown`, written after it. Both fields belong in the agent plan's `WriteReceipt` and in the harness's `code_call` and `tool_result` entries. Then add U7's crash test to A13 or the capstone A15: kill the process between the helpdesk accepting a reply and the result being appended, and check that resume reconciles to `applied` and sends nothing twice. Until that test passes, as U7 puts it, "exactly once" in our plans means "once, if nothing crashes". Ingress deduplication and write keys are two halves of one property. The A24 owner's answer on whether the helpdesk supports webhooks and idempotency keys therefore decides both designs.

**Third, add two error-handling defaults for ablation.** Factor 3 says to "consider hiding errors and failed calls from context window once they are resolved" ([factor 3 L230](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-03-own-your-context-window.md)), and none of our five layers does so. Add an optional layer-2 rule that masks a failed call and its error once a later call to the same tool succeeds. Leave it off by default, and ablate it in N39 next to the other layers ([HD3](../../plans/build-harness.md#hd3-layers-1-2-3-and-5-on-by-default-and-a-budget-capped-at-200k-tokens-provisional)). Separately, D11 counts only identical failures, meaning the same tool with the same input hash. The essay instead suggests limiting a tool to "~3 attempts", and its example stops after three consecutive errors from any call, whatever the input ([factor 9 L31-61](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/d20c728368bf9c189d6d7aab704744decb6ec0cc/content/factor-09-compact-errors.md)). A model that varies its arguments on every failing attempt slips past D11. Because D11 is provisional and "N39 retunes" it ([D11](../../plans/build-sdk.md#d11-three-identical-failing-calls-stop-the-run-and-the-depth-cap-is-2-provisional)), N39 should compare both rules. Neither change needs an ADR.

**Fourth, pre-fetch what intake already holds.** Intake loads the requester's account, plan and orders in code before any model call ([agent guide §2](../../guides/agent.md#2-choose-the-least-autonomy-the-task-allows-a-workflow-in-code-with-one-bounded-loop-for-the-ambiguous-middle)). Yet the planner still carries two T0 lookups for that same data, `support_get_requester` and `support_list_requester_orders`, beside `help_search_articles` ([agent guide §9](../../guides/agent.md#9-reference-architecture)). In A11, put the requester summary and order list into the planner's operator-origin input. Then move the two lookups to `exposure: "deferred"` or drop them, and consider running `help_search_articles` in code from the reader's `category` and `product` ([A11](../../todos/ready/A11-planner-context.md)). This change follows OpenAI's "Don't make the model fill arguments you already know" ([agent guide §3](../../guides/agent.md#3-tools-deserve-more-design-effort-than-the-prompt-few-workflow-shaped-tools-identity-from-trusted-context-and-errors-that-say-what-to-do-next)). It also trims tool definitions, the largest fixed cost in the preamble: deferred loading cut them from about 77K to 8.7K tokens in Anthropic's measurements, as our harness guide reports ([harness guide §4](../../guides/agent-harness.md#4-the-tool-registry-should-name-defer-and-gate-every-tool-from-one-place-and-the-mcp-client-should-be-a-removable-extension)). Keep the change only if A20 shows that it does not hurt cost per resolved ticket or pass^5.

**Fifth, consider a resumable question to an internal person.** Today the agent can ask only the requester a question, and a gap in authority ends in a handoff rather than in a pause that resumes with an answer ([AD12](../../plans/build-agent.md#ad12-a-missing-fact-becomes-a-templated-question-through-the-reply-gate-and-missing-authority-becomes-a-handoff-provisional)). The machinery for such a pause already exists. The harness protocol has a `clarification/request` server request ([HD6](../../plans/build-harness.md#hd6-strict-json-rpc-20-with-the-jsonrpc-field-and-twelve-v1-methods)), and a `Resolution` can carry an `answer` ([build-sdk §3.3](../../plans/build-sdk.md#33-sdk-api)). When AD12 is retuned from shadow labels, test a second clarification target, an internal role such as `support_lead`, so the planner can resume rather than hand off. Adopt it only if shadow data shows handoffs that a one-line internal answer would have resolved.

**Three ideas stay out.** Custom single-message context formats stay out, because they fight our cache rules and no data shows a gain. Generic "wait for an external event" pauses stay out, because the host-tool pattern they need is a harness non-goal ([build-harness §1](../../plans/build-harness.md#1-goal-and-non-goals)); we would revisit it only if a triage step must wait on an asynchronous helpdesk job. A mirror of the essay stays out because of its share-alike licence. Finally, this report closes the gap in our OSS-frameworks notes, which "Could not retrieve … HumanLayer's '12-factor agents' directly" ([oss-frameworks notes, Gaps](../notes/oss-frameworks.md)). Following the fact-check rule in [AGENTS.md](../../../AGENTS.md#every-document-is-drafted-fact-checked-and-reviewed-before-it-merges), we log that closure as a row in the main [fact-check log](../fact-check-2026-11-18.md#later-closures) and leave the note unchanged.

## Conclusion: safety moves to the edges once the agent writes to a helpdesk

12-factor agents is best read as an early practitioner statement of a consensus that has since been measured, not as a source of evidence. Our plans already contain its hardest-won idea, a durable pause between choosing a tool and running it. They harden that pause with signatures, a quorum and policy re-evaluation, none of which the essay considers. Its vendor bias shows in the one place it is wrong for us. The essay treats human approval as the key to high-stakes tools, while our evidence says approvals work only while they stay rare. For triage, the tier table, not the approval channel, is the real control.

The more useful lesson lies in what the essay's simple model leaves out. A thread you can fork and a reducer you can replay are safe only while every effect lives inside the thread. Once the agent writes to a helpdesk it does not own, safety moves to the edges: deduplicated ingress on the way in, and keyed writes with explicit `unknown` outcomes on the way out. Both designs depend on what the helpdesk chosen at A24 supports. **We should therefore write the ingress and effects-ledger designs against the A24 answer before Stage 3's capstone, the first point where a duplicate reply could slip through unnoticed.**
