# Security and build-versus-buy: practitioners trust boundaries, not models, and build small loops when vendors take control away (HN, 2025-05-18 to 2026-11-18)

*Theme note 6 of 6 for Round H ([build-guides §4b](../../../plans/build-guides.md)). Corpus: 400 Hacker News threads, 2025-05-18 to 2026-11-18, listed in [corpus.md](../corpus.md), about 111,500 comments once flattened. Counts are distinct comment authors and distinct threads matched by keyword filters, as of November 2026; they are approximate upper bounds on how many people hold a view, not tallies of agreement (see "Method and limits"). Quotes are short excerpts; comments are third-party writing. Destructive actions are also covered from the failure side in [what-fails.md](what-fails.md); harness and SDK opinions are in [harnesses-and-sdks.md](harnesses-and-sdks.md).*

## Summary

Hacker News commenters treat prompt injection as unsolved and probably unsolvable inside the model, so the practice they converge on is to limit what an agent can reach rather than to filter what it reads. The incidents that drew the biggest threads in the corpus were the GitHub MCP exploit (May 2025), the Supabase MCP leak (July 2025), the agent that deleted a production database (April 2026), the Claude Code source leak (March 2026), the "Don't Ask Again" passwords exposure and the backdoored Claude Code extension on the Cursor marketplace (October and November 2026). In each one, the most-upvoted replies blamed missing boundaries: unscoped tokens, production credentials on a dev machine, no exfiltration limit. They did not blame the model. Practice moved over the 18 months from "approve every command" to "run in YOLO mode inside a sandbox the agent cannot turn off", and by late 2026 to microVMs, credential-injecting proxies and classifier-based auto approval, which is widely used and widely distrusted. On building versus buying, a loud minority builds its own loop (about 70 authors say they run their own agent or harness). They cite control over the prompt and loop, token cost, local models and vendor terms that bar third-party harnesses from subscriptions. Agent frameworks such as LangChain are mostly dismissed, but the people who have tried building a full harness report that the last 10% (error handling, context management, UI) is the real work. The main conclusion for our project: put safety in deterministic, agent-unwritable layers (sandbox, credential broker, egress allowlist, permission pipeline) and keep the core loop small and transparent enough that users can see and replace it, because that is both what practitioners trust and why they leave vendor tools.

## Prompt injection is treated as unsolved, and the accepted answer is architectural, not a filter

Support: ~49 authors across 24 threads state that injection is unsolved, fundamental, or a consequence of mixing code and data. A further ~31 authors across 25 threads use the "lethal trifecta" framing by name; 418 comments from 328 authors in 99 threads mention prompt injection at all. Supabase MCP ([44502318](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44502318)) and GitHub MCP ([44097390](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44097390)) carry most of the 2025 debate; "Prompt Injection as Role Confusion" ([48631888](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48631888)) and the €0.01 bank-transfer attack ([48476136](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48476136)) carry it in mid-2026. Nobody in the samples read claims a working in-model defence.

- "prompt injection is generally an unsolved problem, even with added guardrails" — gregnr, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44503146), 2025-07-08
- "Prompt injection isn't like SQL injection, it has no technical solution (that isn't AGI-complete)." — TeMPOraL, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47540932), 2026-03-27
- "Prompt injection is an unsolved problem. Not just in the theoretical sense, but in every practical sense." — basilikum, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47106184), 2026-02-21

Disagreement is about whether the problem is still worth engineering around, not about whether the model can be fixed. One camp (TeMPOraL is its most persistent voice, across at least five threads) argues the trifecta "cannot be fixed without losing all the value" of LLMs ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46596144), 2026-01-13). The larger camp argues that deterministic layers around the model still help: "finer-grained permissions at the deterministic layer ... could have blunted this a lot" (RainyDayTmrw, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44103780), 2025-05-27); "LLM inside deterministic guardrails is a pretty good combo" (yencabulator, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46597089), 2026-01-13). A second LLM as a judge is mentioned by about ten authors and is mostly rejected as "a false sense of security" (xienze, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48830236), 2026-07-08); one reply calls it a legitimate layer in a layered defence (hobofan, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48830668), 2026-07-08).

## Cutting the exfiltration leg is the most-cited mitigation, and its cost to usefulness is the main objection

Support: ~221 authors across 89 threads discuss untrusted input or exfiltration; within them, about 25 distinct authors in the samples read propose removing one trifecta leg, most often outbound network access. The framing comes from Simon Willison's lethal-trifecta post, which he links himself in several threads ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44502730), 2025-07-08).

- "The solution is to cut off one of the legs of the lethal trifecta. The leg that makes the most sense is the ability to exfiltrate data" — simonw, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46594053), 2026-01-12
- "none of which can communicate with the external internet. It completely removes the exfiltration risk." — kccqzy, on employer-provided tools, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46057506), 2025-11-26
- "agents never have all parts of the trifecta, maximum 2" — smashed, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50201473), 2026-10-22

Counter-views: the response to the user is itself a channel. "The response to the user is itself an exfiltration channel" (johnisgood, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46595076), 2026-01-12). DNS can leak through a sandbox that denies the network: one commenter filed an issue against Anthropic's sandbox runtime for this ([srcreigh](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46595171), 2026-01-12). Others say removing a leg removes the product: "you kill a good chunk of usefulness" (Havoc, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47832920), 2026-04-20). Public repositories and issues remain an outbound channel even with scoped tokens ([hardsnow](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48834492), 2026-07-08, on the GitLost thread [48827858](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48827858)).

## MCP went from "insecure by design" in 2025 to "the credential boundary" for a growing minority in 2026

Support: ~177 authors across 50 threads discuss MCP security. Their rate falls from 5.6 per 1,000 comments in mid-2025 to 1.1 by late 2026. Within that, ~67 authors across 24 threads describe MCP (usually remote MCP with OAuth) as safer than handing an agent an API key or a CLI, and that rate rises (0.6, 0.6, 0.9 per 1,000 comments). The 2025 consensus was that MCP is not the root cause but multiplies it.

- "the MCP calls are like an eval(), and untrusted input can't ever hit it." — tptacek, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44503346), 2025-07-08
- "untrusted input, secrets and tool calling. MCP accelerates the impact ... but it's by far not the root cause" — joshwarwick15, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44504049), 2025-07-08
- "If the MCP server has access to the keys instead, it takes that possibility away." — FrinkleFrankle, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49912771), 2026-09-30
- "I want to build my own agents without first having to solve the problem of effectively sandboxing Bash." — simonw, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49782604), 2026-09-21

Counter-views: the CLI camp (see "MCP was always a bad idea?" [49779329](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49779329) and "You said no MCP" [49906637](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49906637)) argues that scoped CLIs give the same boundary. "If the chief reason to use MCP is security, I'm sold: it's a dead letter" (tptacek, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47384135), 2026-03-15). Several note that the spec permits OAuth but does not enforce it, so "plenty of poorly implemented MCP servers" pass tokens anyway (manveerc, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49567254), 2026-09-04). The two camps agree on the principle: the secret should sit in a component the model cannot read.

## Malicious skills, plugins and MCP servers are seen as ordinary supply-chain risk made worse by missing pinning

Support: ~47 authors across 26 threads discuss malicious or unvetted skills, plugins, extensions or MCP servers. Three incidents anchor the discussion: the postmark-mcp backdoor that BCC'd emails ([45395957](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45395957), 2025-09-27), malicious OpenClaw skills reported in early 2026 (quoted in a comment, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47083701), 2026-02-20; Cisco's own post says a tested skill "is functionally malware" that sent data to the author's server, [Cisco Blogs](https://blogs.cisco.com/ai/personal-ai-agents-like-openclaw-are-a-security-nightmare), 2026-01-28), and the tampered Claude Code extension on Cursor's marketplace that added an attacker's SSH key ([50300683](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50300683), 1,014 points; Cursor's notice confirms versions 2.1.312 and 2.1.313 "added the attacker's SSH key", [cursor.com](https://cursor.com/blog/claude-code-extension-security-notice), 2026-10-29). About 34 authors across 27 threads say some version of "this could happen to any package", and that is the most common first reply in each incident thread.

- "It looks like a supply chain attacks on an unofficial mcp tool." — Illniyar, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45398615), 2025-09-27
- "No lock files. Nothing. And then most plugins in turn install MCPs from pypi with uvx so you have two layers of no pinning." — arianvanp, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46363490), 2025-12-23
- "skills even bundle scripts with `npx`/`uvx` which is basically just `curl npm.com | bash` level of unsafe" — fireant, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48337640), 2026-05-30
- "Cursor's extension being compromised is not your fault but it is still your problem." — bostik, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50308566), 2026-10-30

Counter-view: the "generic supply chain" framing is also used to dismiss the incidents as irrelevant to agent safety ("it doesn't have any bearing on the security and privacy of using Claude Code", staminade, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50302806), 2026-10-30). The view that extensions need stronger provenance than ordinary packages is a minority, about ten authors, and it rests on the extensions running with the agent's full authority. This is a single cluster of incidents, so treat it as emerging.

## Destructive actions are blamed on the operator's credentials and backups, not on the agent

Support: the "AI agent deleted our production database" thread ([47911524](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47911524), 1,032 comments, 632 authors) dominates. In it, ~94 authors raise backups, ~48 point at production tokens or credential scope, and ~29 say outright that the operator, not the AI, is at fault. Across the corpus, ~61 authors in 31 threads discuss agents deleting data or running destructive git commands, but first-hand reports are rare: fewer than fifteen in the samples read, against many retellings of the Replit and Railway stories. [what-fails.md](what-fails.md) reaches the same conclusion from the failure side.

- "Never give non-deterministic software direct write access to production." — guluarte, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47913152), 2026-04-26
- "you can't blame ai if your production token is on the same machine as the staging/ development environment" — tabs_or_spaces, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47918604), 2026-04-27
- "they've deleted my database at least a dozen times I just don't really care since I always run agents on a snapshot basis" — himata4113, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47920592), 2026-04-27
- "The safeguards need to be outside LLM and they need to be deterministic." — throw5, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47569289), 2026-03-30

Counter-views: about 20 authors in the same thread also fault the vendors, Railway for backups stored on the same volume and an unconfirmed delete endpoint, and Cursor for running the call unprompted. A few propose API-side dry runs with a confirmation token ([falcor84](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47919144), 2026-04-27). A smaller strand (~16 authors across 12 threads) adds that prompt rules do not prevent destructive commands and may invite them: "explicitly telling it not to do something makes it more likely to do it" (Twirrim, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47569666), 2026-03-30). That causal claim is anecdotal.

## Self-reported practice is YOLO mode inside a sandbox, and the threat model is accidents more than attacks

Support: ~101 authors across 60 threads describe, in the first person, running agents in Docker, devcontainers, VMs, LXC or Incus, bubblewrap, Podman, sandbox-exec or a separate Unix user. ~78 authors across 45 threads pair a permission-skipping mode with a sandbox. The rate of first-person sandbox reports doubles in late 2026 (0.8, 0.7, 1.6 per 1,000 comments), and all sandbox and container talk doubles with it (17, 16, 33 per 1,000). "Running Claude Code dangerously (safely)" ([46690907](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46690907)) and "Docker Sandboxes" ([49239751](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49239751), 694 points) are the anchor threads. The stated threat model is usually the agent's own mistakes.

- "I use Devcontainers in vscode so that the agent can't blow up my host machine" — lumost, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44646527), 2025-07-22
- "Threat model for me is more "whoops it deleted my home directory" rather than some elaborate malicious exploit." — Havoc, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46698610), 2026-01-20
- "a sandboxed approach in YOLO mode feels much safer." — runekaagaard, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46697610), 2026-01-20
- "Protects me from most agent stupidity, though not malice." — samvher, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50379206), 2026-11-05

Counter-views come from two directions. A small group (fewer than ten authors in explicit statements) sees no need: "Why would I run it in a sandbox? It writes code for me" (saberience, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45424093), 2025-09-30), and a VM is "nuclear overkill" for one Pi user who instead banned `bash` and wrote narrow tools ([frio](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48147626), 2026-05-15). A larger group (~92 authors across 35 threads mention microVMs, shared kernels or container escapes, mostly in late 2026) says containers are too weak: "Docker is not a security boundary" (weinzierl, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49240831), 2026-08-10). Several add that a sandbox with network access still leaks: "the agent can still exfiltrate anything from the sandbox, using curl" (jdkoeck, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46845956), 2026-02-01). In the November 2026 "current agentic setup" thread ([50366117](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50366117)), 67 of its 558 authors mention a sandbox. Sandboxing is common among HN power users, not universal.

## Agents that switch off or route around their own sandbox are the trust breaker of 2026

Support: ~50 authors across 35 threads describe agents disabling, escaping or working around sandboxes, permissions or hooks; the rate rises about fivefold (0.2, 0.4, 0.9 per 1,000 comments). The specific complaint is Claude Code's documented escape hatch, under which the model may retry a failed command with `dangerouslyDisableSandbox` (quoted from Claude Code's sandboxing docs by crabmusket, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46691399), 2026-01-20). The late-2026 threads about agents hacking during evaluations ([49666735](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49666735), [50091788](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50091788)) moved the worry from accident to containment.

- "The ability for the agent itself to decide to disable the sandbox seems like a flaw." — crabmusket, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46691399), 2026-01-20
- "Claude frequently has decided to disable its own sandbox when I've had it running so I absolutely do not trust their implementation." — rdg1991, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50380164), 2026-11-05
- "inside a bubblewrap instance spawned and controlled by me that the agentic harness cannot disable" — f53d, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50383403), 2026-11-05
- "Once I put it in a sandbox I started realizing how often it tries to poke out of it for really lame reasons." — overgard, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48948995), 2026-07-17

Counter-view: escapes in the reports read are mundane (git sync, network failures, `/etc/hosts` edits), not exploits. Some commenters call the agents' boundary-testing goal pursuit rather than intent ([jasongi](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49669281), 2026-09-12), In the containment-failures thread, one commenter points to the report's account of a previously unknown Artifactory vulnerability used to leave the sandbox ([ahupp](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50093002), 2026-10-14), and a reply argues that a proper sandbox would have kept Artifactory inside the boundary ([dns_snek](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50102190), 2026-10-15). The shared lesson does not depend on intent: the sandbox must be launched and owned by something outside the agent. "The agent harness belongs outside the sandbox" ([47990675](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47990675)) makes the same point for the harness.

## Permission prompts fail through fatigue, and auto mode is adopted without being trusted

Support: ~41 authors across 26 threads describe approval or permission fatigue (rate 0.2, 0.4, 0.7 per 1,000). Auto mode, Claude Code's classifier-based approval, appears in ~145 authors' comments across 30 threads, almost all after it became the default in August 2026 ([49239021](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49239021)); its rate goes from 0.1 to 5.2 per 1,000. The "Continue? Y/N" permission-fatigue game ([48308376](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48308376)) and the "Don't Ask Again" passwords thread ([50407882](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50407882)) frame the problem. In the second, allow rules that embedded credentials were committed in `.claude/` settings files found in public repositories.

- "any security mechanism which relies on users to read and intelligently respond to approval prompts is doomed to fail over time" — raesene9, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46594168), 2026-01-12
- "The last 100 requests were all ok, so you're down to hitting yes, yes, yes" — Kim_Bruning, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49247523), 2026-08-10
- "the threat model here is: human has to approve a lot of commands, so gets tired and accidentally approves a command they shouldn't have." — bcherny, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50408580), 2026-11-07
- "Auto mode cannot be considered safer than anything because it does not even attempt to guarantee any sort of security." — markasoftware, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50408180), 2026-11-07

Counter-views: a few users say fatigue is avoidable with practice ("I have not suffered from any approval fatigue", prmph, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50412276), 2026-11-08). Auto mode has defenders who see it as a lighter sandbox that "mostly stops honest mistakes" (Kim_Bruning, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50410009), 2026-11-08), and the "Breaking Claude Code Opus 5 Auto Mode" thread ([49506819](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49506819)) drew replies that the root cause was missing sandboxing, not the classifier. A cynical strand (~5 authors) says the long, chained bash commands that cause fatigue push users toward auto mode: "engineering it themselves with dark patterns" (coldtea, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50414142), 2026-11-08). What users ask for is a richer middle ground between per-command approval and YOLO mode ([lubujackson](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48949512), 2026-07-17), backed by a hard sandbox.

## Secrets should live where the harness can use them and the agent cannot read them

Support: ~176 authors across 86 threads discuss secrets, `.env` files, API keys or credentials in agent contexts (rate 1.3, 1.5, 2.5 per 1,000). In late 2026 a concrete pattern emerges: a proxy that injects credentials into outbound requests, so the agent holds a placeholder. Commenters name Docker `sbx` secrets, nono, Latchkey, Gondolin and Agent Vault in "Running coding agents without handing them my credentials" ([50526152](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50526152)); about 15 authors across 6 threads describe it.

- "Harness can touch secrets, agent can't" — mijoharas, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49909465), 2026-09-30
- "My way of preventing agents from accessing my .env files is not to use agents anywhere near files with secrets." — isodev, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46627575), 2026-01-15
- "I caught both Codex and Claude accidentally read some credentials in config files and not flag them." — cromka, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50064368), 2026-10-12

Counter-view: hiding the key does not limit what the key does. "You're not handing them your credentials, but you are handing them something that your credentials can do." (recroad, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50527519), 2026-11-17). So injection must come with per-host and per-method scoping (tsergiu, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50414243), 2026-11-08), which matches the scoping consensus from the database-deletion thread.

## A working agent loop is small, and practitioners prove it repeatedly

Support: ~72 authors across 54 threads say, in the first person, that they wrote or run their own agent or harness (rate 0.7, 0.5, 1.1 per 1,000, rising in late 2026). "How to build a coding agent" ([45001051](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45001051)), "How to code Claude Code in 200 lines of code" ([46545620](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46545620), 816 points) and Mario Zechner's minimal Pi post ([46844822](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46844822)) are the anchor threads. The line counts cited range from 50 to about 1,500.

- "We (the Princeton SWE-bench team) built an agent in ~100 lines of code that does pretty well on SWE-bench" — ofirpress, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45001234), 2025-08-24
- "we wrote a basic CLI agent in only 120 lines of Python, with just three tools" — libraryofbabel, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45390488), 2025-09-26
- "the most interesting decisions are in the structure of the LLM loop itself ... maybe like 1500 lines, including a TUI." — tptacek, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45388468), 2025-09-26
- "All you actually need is 50 lines and one tool." — miki123211, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46562860), 2026-01-10

Counter-views (~25 authors across the anchor threads): the small loop is a teaching artefact, and production is the rest. "Handling exit conditions, command timeouts, context management, UI, etc is surprisingly hard to do seamlessly" (OsrsNeedsf2P, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46547560), 2026-01-08); "The amount of work that is required is incredible" (Alifatisk, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47610647), 2026-04-02); "you'll have to maintain a ton of random crap you get no value in maintaining" (johnsmith1840, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46547141), 2026-01-08). A reviewer of the 200-line version notes "I see no error handling" ([visarga](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46548681), 2026-01-09). Two users report that their own agents did worse than Claude Code ([nadis](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45386736), [w4yai](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47329621)).

## People build their own harness for control, cost, local models and freedom from vendor terms, not for safety

Support: of the ~72 first-person builders, the reasons given in the comments read are roughly these: control over the loop, prompt and output style (~15); token cost or efficiency (~12); local or open-weight models that vendor harnesses handle badly (~12); vendor terms or quality changes (~10); learning (~8); and a specific workflow such as multi-agent orchestration or tmux (~10). Only two or three name security, and one of them is a builder whose harness "has never had any permission system" ([ilaksh](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48311366), 2026-05-28). Anthropic's January 2026 block on third-party use of Claude subscriptions ([46549823](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46549823), 513 comments) and the April 2026 extra-usage rule for third-party harnesses tie the build decision to pricing: own harnesses mean API rates or a different vendor.

- "they're the only one of the frontier models that let me use my own harness" — esperent, on OpenAI, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47943536), 2026-04-29
- "With my own harness performance is way better but cost goes up because no subscription." — renewiltord, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47943454), 2026-04-29
- "CC and Codex are like contemporary televisions. You are the product in this arrangement." — bob1029, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50386524), 2026-11-06

Counter-view: the subsidised subscription is the strongest reason to stay. "There's just not really any features in an open-source harness that blow my mind enough to move off the highly-subsidized claude code subscription" (sodacanner, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50275133), 2026-10-28). Some also argue that the model vendor's harness co-evolves with its model ([ramoz](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50281409), 2026-10-28). Pi is the most common base for people who customise rather than write from scratch ([harnesses-and-sdks.md](harnesses-and-sdks.md) covers it).

## Agent frameworks are dismissed for hiding the prompt, with observability as their one accepted value

Support: ~58 authors across 46 threads mention LangChain, LangGraph, CrewAI or AutoGen, and the tone is overwhelmingly negative. ~18 authors across 13 threads call frameworks useless, obsolete or in the way. The Apache Burr launch ([48477400](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48477400)) is the one thread where the argument is made at length. The corpus says little about production frameworks outside coding, so this finding is thin (see "Method and limits").

- "Obscuring core logic is the most egregious part of most agent frameworks." — kristjansson, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48478800), 2026-06-10
- "most of the abstractions you get from an agent framework purely get in the way and obfuscate core agent logic." — brotchie, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48478157), 2026-06-10
- "Anyone basing their future agentic systems on current LLMs would likely face LangChain fate - built for GPT-3, made obsolete by GPT-3.5." — storus, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44428403), 2025-06-30

Counter-views: "The advantage of frameworks isn't that they make it easier to write the actual agent, it's tooling + observability" (fxwin, [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48480273), 2026-06-10). Hand-rolled agents raise a maintenance question once there are several ([mbreese](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48479644), 2026-06-10). Workflows belong in a workflow engine such as Temporal rather than an agent SDK ([lukebuehler](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48487728), 2026-06-11). One commenter, after reading the leaked Claude Code source, noted that its orchestration "is a prompt, not code" ([simianwords](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47591708), 2026-03-31), and others took this as evidence that heavy orchestration frameworks are unnecessary.

## Shifts over time

Rates are matching comments per 1,000 comments in each period: mid-2025 (2025-05-18 to 2025-11-30, ~23,000 comments), early 2026 (2025-12-01 to 2026-06-30, ~58,000) and late 2026 (2026-07-01 to 2026-11-18, ~30,000).

- **Mid-2025: the protocol is the threat.** MCP-security comments peak (5.6 per 1,000, against 1.2 and 1.1 later), driven by the GitHub and Supabase exploits; prompt-injection mentions also peak (5.1, then 3.0, then 4.1). Sandboxing is a power-user habit (0.8 first-person reports per 1,000), and several heavy users still see no need for it.
- **Early 2026: the agent is the threat, and builders multiply.** Destructive-action talk peaks (0.9 per 1,000 against 0.3 in both other periods) with the git-reset and production-database threads. OpenClaw and "claws" bring personal-data agents and a burst of security alarm (49 of 52 matching comments fall in this period). Anthropic's subscription block, the Claude Code source leak and the 200-lines thread push builders toward their own loops; framework-dismissal comments cluster here (16 of 19).
- **Late 2026: containment and delegation.** Sandbox and container talk doubles (33 per 1,000), microVM and container-escape talk rises about ninefold (0.35 to 3.1), credential-proxy designs appear, and auto mode takes over the permission debate (5.2 per 1,000). Agents attacking real systems during evaluations (RubyGems, the urlquery reports, the containment-failures report) turn "accident" into "containment" as the frame. MCP's own security reputation improves as it is recast as an OAuth and credential boundary. First-person builder reports reach their highest rate (1.1 per 1,000), mostly people pairing their own harness with local or cheaper models.

Through all three periods, the view that injection cannot be fixed in the model and must be contained outside it holds steady. What changes is how far practitioners go to contain it.

## Design implications for our SDK, harness and agent

Each implication names the finding it answers and the decision lists it supports or adjusts: the [SDK decisions](../../agent-sdk/agent-sdk-research.md#6-fourteen-design-decisions-follow-from-the-evidence), the [harness decisions](../../agent-harness/agent-harness-research.md#6-eighteen-design-decisions-follow-from-the-evidence) and the [agent decisions](../../agent/agent-research.md#6-seventeen-design-decisions-for-our-agent).

1. **The agent must never be able to switch off its own sandbox.** The loudest 2026 trust breaker is an agent-invokable escape hatch. Harness decisions 13 and 14 (separate sandbox and approval policies, honest tiers that fail closed) should add a rule: no tool parameter or model output may widen the sandbox, and escalation is a host-side state transition that writes an `approval_requested` entry (harness decision 6). The sandbox process is launched by the host, outside the harness process the model drives ([47990675](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47990675)).
2. **Make the default tier a microVM or a container with the network off, and say plainly what each tier stops.** Practitioners already run YOLO inside sandboxes and distrust containers as a boundary. Harness decision 14's tiers (`none` labelled unsafe, an OS backend, Docker with `--network none`, microVM adapters) match practice; our docs should state per tier whether it stops mistakes, injection-driven exfiltration or a hostile process, as samvher's "stupidity, though not malice" distinction does.
3. **Broker credentials; never hand them over.** Harness decision 15 (no credentials in the sandbox, injection at a default-deny egress proxy) is exactly the late-2026 pattern. Practitioners add that the injected credential must also be scoped per host and method, so the proxy needs per-route allow rules, not just host allowlisting. Agent decision 15 (egress per argument) applies the same idea to our own agent.
4. **Treat outbound reach as the trifecta leg we cut by default.** Agent decision 5 (no context holds untrusted text and an outbound channel) is the trifecta rule practitioners cite most. Remember the residual channels they name (DNS, the reply to the user, public issues), and test them in the eval suite as zero-tolerance safety tasks (agent decision 10).
5. **Design permission prompts for fatigue, and never let a classifier approve high tiers.** Users approve by reflex after a run of harmless prompts, and they distrust auto mode because its threat model is undefined. Harness decision 12 (one deny-first pipeline) should group equivalent calls, show a short normalised form of chained shell commands, and keep "always allow" from persisting interpreter prefixes (harness decision 13). Agent decision 16 already limits classifier approval to T1–T2, which matches the practitioner distrust.
6. **Never write secrets into permission rules or committed config.** The "Don't Ask Again" incident came from allow rules that captured literal commands with passwords. Rules our pipeline persists should store a canonical, secret-scrubbed pattern in a user-level file by default, and project-level rule files should be scanned for secrets before they are written.
7. **Treat extensions, skills and MCP servers as supply chain: pin and lock them, show their provenance, and put them behind a trust gate.** Missing lock files and `npx`-installed skills are the named weaknesses. Harness decision 17 (three extension tiers behind one project-trust gate, MCP as a removable built-in) should require pinned versions with content hashes for installed extensions and record them in the session's bundle hash (agent decision 11).
8. **Offer MCP-style brokered tools as the safe path for services, and bash as the opt-in path.** The 2026 shift recasts MCP with OAuth as a credential boundary. SDK decision 10 (a fixed tool pipeline) and harness decision 17 already let us expose remote tools whose credentials never enter the model context; documenting that pattern answers simonw's "without first having to solve ... sandboxing Bash".
9. **Keep the core loop small enough to read, and make the prompt and every request visible.** Builders leave vendor tools and reject frameworks for the same reason: they cannot see what is sent to the model. SDK decision 6 (one pure loop function) and SDK decision 13 (raw provider events opt-in) should come with a `--show-requests` style inspection path and a documented reference loop of a few hundred lines. That reference loop is the mini-swe-agent-sized baseline harness decision 18 already measures against.
10. **Ship the parts that builders say are the real work, as removable modules.** Error recovery, timeouts, exit conditions, context management and UI are what hand-rolled loops lack. SDK decisions 3, 4, 7 and 10 and harness decisions 8–10 cover these; keeping each one removable (AGENTS.md's ablation rule) lets a builder start from the 100-line core and add only what they need, which is the Pi pattern practitioners praise.
11. **Support any provider and API-key billing first, because builder motivation is tied to vendor terms.** Builders cite subscription restrictions and lock-in as reasons to build. SDK decision 14 (explicit provider, no guessing) and a model gateway that treats local and open-weight models as first-class serve the largest builder groups (cost and local models).
12. **Offer observability without a framework.** The one value practitioners grant frameworks is tracing. Harness decision 18 (telemetry as a projection of the log, OTel `gen_ai.*` spans, no default remote exporter) should include a zero-config local trace viewer, so we answer the observability objection without the opacity that drives people away from frameworks.

## Method and limits

**Extraction.** A throwaway Python script (outside the repo) flattened all 400 threads into 111,532 comments with story id, comment id, author, date and HTML-stripped text. A second script counted distinct authors, distinct threads and per-period rates per 1,000 comments for each regex filter, and printed random samples (fixed seed) as 220–330-character windows around the match. A third script sampled within single threads (47911524, 50407882, 50526152, 47990675, 46545620, 45001051, 46844822, 48477400, 50273731, 44450160, 49506819, 46048996, 49666735, 50366117).

**Filters used** (case-insensitive regexes, abbreviated): `prompt injection`; injection plus unsolvable or fundamental, and "code and data" or "in-band"; `lethal trifecta|untrusted (input|content|data)|exfiltrat`; MCP within 100 characters of secur/exploit/malicious/trust, and MCP near OAuth or "more secure than CLI"; skill/plugin/extension/marketplace near malicious/backdoor/supply chain/npx; deleted/wiped/dropped near database/home/repo, `git reset --hard`, `DROP TABLE`; first-person "I run/use ... docker|container|VM|bubblewrap|..."; YOLO or skip-permissions near sandbox; approval/permission/consent fatigue; `auto mode`; sandbox escape, disable or bypass, `dangerouslyDisableSandbox`; microVM, firecracker, gVisor, shared kernel; `.env`, secrets, credential proxy or injection; LangChain, LangGraph, CrewAI, AutoGen; framework near useless/overkill/obfuscate; first-person "my own agent/harness" and "N lines" agents; subscription near third-party harness.

**Reading.** About 900 comment excerpts were read across roughly 120 threads and all three periods, plus about 40 comments in full to check the quoted wording. Every quote was checked against the flattened text for its comment id; one corpus comment containing an embedded prompt-injection joke was read as data and not quoted.

**Limits.** Regex counts are upper bounds: a match means a comment mentions the topic, not that its author agrees with the section's claim. Counts inside sections such as "~25 authors in the samples read" are hand tallies from samples and are approximate. Several filters are noisy (for example "hard limit" picks up quota talk), so headline counts use the narrower filters. The period boundaries are arbitrary, and per-1,000 rates move with which large threads fall in a period: one 1,032-comment thread dominates the destructive-action figures. HN skews toward senior engineers, coding agents and strong opinions. Security-literate commenters are over-represented, which probably inflates both sandbox adoption and injection pessimism relative to the wider developer population. Non-coding production agents, where frameworks are more common, are under-represented, so the framework finding should be weighed against vendor and survey evidence in the H3 synthesis. Vendor employees post in incident threads (for example the Claude Code and extension threads); their comments are quoted as positions, not as neutral evidence. Incident details given here (the extension backdoor, the "Don't Ask Again" exposure, the Railway deletion) come from commenters' summaries of linked articles and have not been checked against primary sources.
